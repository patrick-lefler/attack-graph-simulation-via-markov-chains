Multi-Stage Attack Graph Simulation Via Markov Chains: the Fundamental Matrix, the assumptions, the solver, and the diagnostic plots

Mathematical Foundation: The Fundamental Matrix Framework

By organizing all attack states into a standardized matrix, the model separates transient stages (where the attacker is actively maneuvering) from absorbing states (permanent outcomes). Through closed-form matrix inversion, it computes the expected time spent in each stage and the probability of each terminal outcome, conditional on the assumed transition matrix.

Canonical Block Form

Let the state space contain \(t\) transient states and \(r\) absorbing states (\(n = t + r\)). The transition probability matrix \(P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) is partitioned into canonical block form:

\[P = \begin{pmatrix} Q & R \\ \mathbf{0} & I_r \end{pmatrix}\]

  • \(Q \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times t}\): Transition probabilities between transient states.
  • \(R \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times r}\): Transition probabilities from transient states into absorbing states.
  • \(\mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times t}\): Zero matrix (absorbing states cannot transition back to transient states).
  • \(I_r \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times r}\): Identity matrix of order \(r\) (absorbing states are permanent).

The Fundamental Matrix (\(N\))

If every transient state can reach an absorbing state, \((I_t - Q)\) is invertible. The Fundamental Matrix \(N \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times t}\) is:

\[N = (I_t - Q)^{-1} = I_t + Q + Q^2 + Q^3 + \dots = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} Q^k\]

Each entry \(N_{ij}\) is the expected number of time steps spent in transient state \(j\), given that the attack starts in transient state \(i\). Because \(N = I + Q + \dots\), the starting step counts: \(N_{ii} \ge 1\).

Expected Time to Resolution (\(\mathbf{t}\))

The expected number of steps before the chain is absorbed, from any starting state, is

\[\mathbf{t} = N \mathbf{1}, \qquad t_i = \sum_{j=1}^t N_{ij}\]

This is the expected time to resolution of any kind, including containment. A shorter time to resolution can mean faster containment (good) or faster detonation (bad), so it is not by itself a measure of adversary dwell. Each stage \(j\) has its own mean step duration \(h_j\) (hours), giving

\[\mathbf{T} = N \mathbf{h}\]

In this document every \(h_j = \Delta\tau = 4\) hours, so \(\mathbf{T} = 4\,\mathbf{t}\). The 4-hour step is an assumption. Rescaling hours to a different step is not a valid sensitivity check, because per-step probabilities only have meaning for the step length they were elicited for: a 1-hour step with the same \(P\) would imply a SOC that contains attacks four times faster per hour. A continuous-time formulation (rates per hour) removes this coupling.

The two layers of the model separate cleanly. The absorption probabilities \(B\) depend only on the embedded jump chain, meaning each transient row with its self-loop removed and the remaining entries renormalized. The self-loops and \(\Delta\tau\) affect timing only. The code below verifies this.

Absorption Probabilities (\(B\))

The matrix \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times r}\) gives the probability that an attack starting in transient state \(i\) ends in absorbing state \(k\):

\[B = N R, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^r B_{ik} = 1 \;\; \text{for all } i\]

Conditional Time to Each Outcome

The expected time to resolution, conditional on ending in outcome \(k\), has the closed form

\[E[T \mid \text{start } i,\ \text{absorbed in } k] = \frac{\left(N\,(\mathbf{h} \circ B_{\cdot k})\right)_i}{B_{ik}}\]

where \(\circ\) is the elementwise product. For a catastrophic outcome, \(B_{\cdot k}\) is replaced by \(B_{\cdot A2} + B_{\cdot A3}\). This is the time metric that bears on response timelines, because it separates “time until containment” from “time until a catastrophic outcome, given that one occurs.”

Parameterization and Assumptions

The model has six transient states (kill-chain-style stages) and three absorbing states (outcomes). Transition probabilities are expert-elicited, illustrative values. They are not calibrated to any cited benchmark, and they were typed in by hand: no asset graph, PageRank, CVSS weighting, or loss distribution is computed. Each transient row gives the per-step probability, from a given stage, of staying (self-loop), advancing, regressing, or ending in an absorbing outcome. The baseline and the improved-posture matrices are two hand-set matrices; the improved posture is not derived from any specific control (MFA, EDR, or Zero Trust).

Assumptions that the results depend on:

  • Markov property. “Starting at \(S_i\)” and “having reached \(S_i\)” are treated as identical, and the per-step detection hazard is constant. An attacker who reached \(S_3\) after 30 hours of slow movement has the same future as one who arrived in 4 hours; real detection probability can build up with time and noise.
  • Geometric holding times and a single step length. Each stage has a geometric holding time with a step length of \(\Delta\tau = 4\) hours for all stages. Because the self-loops are small (0.05–0.15), an attacker leaves each stage after a single step 85–95% of the time. The model’s timing is therefore close to deterministic, and no catastrophic outcome can occur in under 16 hours (see Time-to-Outcome Distributions). Real stages differ by orders of magnitude, from sub-hour lateral movement to multi-week dwell, so the implied time scale is a hypothetical, not a benchmark. The per-step probabilities are tied to the 4-hour step, so changing the step without re-eliciting them is not a valid sensitivity check.
  • S1 entry event. S1 begins when a user engages with a phishing lure (opens a malicious attachment, follows a malicious link, or submits credentials), not when a message is delivered. Filtered and ignored messages are outside the model. Under the baseline, 72% of S1 attacks eventually reach S2 (0.65 / 0.90). That is plausible only for this engaged cohort and would be far too high for delivered messages. Time spent in S1 is time to resolution, not dwell time in the industry sense, because the adversary is not yet inside the environment.
  • Outcome space. “Contained / Terminated” (A1) includes automated filtering, user non-engagement, abandoned attacks, and SOC action; it should not be read as “SOC wins.” A2 and A3 are mutually exclusive here, although double extortion (exfiltrate, then encrypt) is common; “catastrophic” counts only the first catastrophic outcome, and the A2/A3 split is not meaningful by itself. A1 is also treated as permanent, although containment is often partial and adversaries can re-enter through persistence or harvested credentials, so containment probabilities are upper bounds on durable containment.
  • Graph structure. Only S5 and S6 can reach A2/A3, and S6 has no regression edge, so catastrophic probability is largely P(reach S5/S6) times the S5/S6 exit split. The single back-edge S4 → S3 (0.05) is an illustrative attacker-backtracking edge with no empirical basis, kept in both regimes. The zeros from S2–S4 into A2 are also a strong claim: data theft from file shares or SaaS does not require domain dominance.
  • Containment share by depth. Conditional on leaving a stage, the share of exits that go to A1 falls from about 0.28–0.29 at S1–S2 to about 0.10–0.11 at S5–S6. That encodes a claim that defenders get worse at stopping attacks as the attacks become noisier. Deep stages usually generate the richest telemetry; what shrinks is the time left to respond. A continuous-time formulation, in which detection and progression are competing rates, can reproduce falling containment at depth without this assumption.
  • Single entry vector. Phishing is the only way in. Exploits were the most common initial infection vector in Mandiant’s 2024 investigations, at 33% (M-Trends 2025). In 56% of Sophos’s 2024 cases, adversaries logged in with valid credentials rather than breaking in (Sophos). Valid-credential entry would skip S1 and start at S2 or S3.

matrix_from_params <- function(rows, transient, absorbing) {
  all_states <- c(transient, absorbing)
  stopifnot(length(rows) == length(transient), all(lengths(rows) == length(all_states)))
  P <- matrix(0, length(all_states), length(all_states), dimnames = list(all_states, all_states))
  P[transient, ] <- do.call(rbind, map(rows, unlist))
  P[absorbing, absorbing] <- diag(length(absorbing))
  P
}

Pure R Algebraic Solver: Fundamental Matrix & Time to Resolution

The solver computes \(N\), expected time to resolution, absorption probabilities, and conditional times to each outcome. Before inverting, it checks that every transient state can reach an absorbing state (which guarantees \(I - Q\) is invertible) and, after computing \(B\), that every row of \(B\) sums to 1.

Display code 
# TRUE if every transient state has a path to an absorbing state
all_reach_absorbing <- function(Q, R) {
  reach <- rowSums(R) > 0
  repeat {
    updated <- reach | as.vector((Q > 0) %*% reach) > 0
    if (identical(updated, reach)) break
    reach <- updated
  }
  all(reach)
}

solve_absorbing_markov <- function(Q, R, h) {
  stopifnot(all_reach_absorbing(Q, R))

  N <- solve(diag(nrow(Q)) - Q, tol = 1e-14)
  dimnames(N) <- dimnames(Q)

  B <- N %*% R
  stopifnot(all(abs(rowSums(B) - 1) < 1e-8))

  # Conditional expected time to each outcome: N (h * B_k) / B_k
  time_given_outcome <- apply(B, 2, \(b) as.vector(N %*% (h * b)) / b)
  rownames(time_given_outcome) <- rownames(Q)

  cat_prob <- B[, 2] + B[, 3]
  time_given_catastrophe <- as.vector(N %*% (h * cat_prob)) / cat_prob

  list(
    N = N,
    B = B,
    steps = rowSums(N),
    hours = as.vector(N %*% h) |> setNames(rownames(Q)),
    time_given_outcome = time_given_outcome,
    time_given_catastrophe = setNames(time_given_catastrophe, rownames(Q))
  )
}
base_solution <- solve_absorbing_markov(Q_base, R_base, step_hours)
hard_solution <- solve_absorbing_markov(Q_hard, R_hard, step_hours)

# B depends only on the embedded jump chain: remove self-loops and renormalize
jump_chain <- function(P) {
  for (i in seq_len(t_dim)) {
    s <- P[i, i]
    P[i, i] <- 0
    P[i, ] <- P[i, ] / (1 - s)
  }
  P
}

Time-to-Outcome Distributions and the Structural Floor

Means hide the shape of the timing. The cumulative incidence function (CIF) for outcome \(k\), starting from stage \(i\), is the probability that the attack has ended in \(k\) by step \(s\):

\[\mathrm{CIF}_k(s) = \sum_{u=0}^{s-1} \mathbf{e}_i^\top Q^u R_{\cdot k}\]

It is exact in closed form from matrix powers, with no simulation needed.

# Probability of absorption into each outcome at each step, by matrix powers
outcome_pmf <- function(Q, R, start = 1, max_steps = 200) {
  x <- replace(numeric(nrow(Q)), start, 1)
  out <- matrix(0, max_steps, ncol(R), dimnames = list(NULL, colnames(R)))
  for (s in seq_len(max_steps)) {
    out[s, ] <- as.vector(x %*% R)
    x <- as.vector(x %*% Q)
  }
  out
}

## Mathematical Foundation: The Fundamental Matrix Framework

By organizing all attack states into a standardized matrix, the model separates transient stages (where the attacker is actively maneuvering) from absorbing states (permanent outcomes). Through closed-form matrix inversion, it computes the expected time spent in each stage and the probability of each terminal outcome, conditional on the assumed transition matrix.

### Canonical Block Form

Let the state space contain $t$ transient states and $r$ absorbing states ($n = t + r$). The transition probability matrix $P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is partitioned into canonical block form:

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} Q & R \\ \mathbf{0} & I_r \end{pmatrix}$$

-   $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times t}$: Transition probabilities between transient states.
-   $R \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times r}$: Transition probabilities from transient states into absorbing states.
-   $\mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times t}$: Zero matrix (absorbing states cannot transition back to transient states).
-   $I_r \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times r}$: Identity matrix of order $r$ (absorbing states are permanent).

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### The Fundamental Matrix ($N$)

If every transient state can reach an absorbing state, $(I_t - Q)$ is invertible. The **Fundamental Matrix** $N \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times t}$ is:

$$N = (I_t - Q)^{-1} = I_t + Q + Q^2 + Q^3 + \dots = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} Q^k$$

Each entry $N_{ij}$ is the **expected number of time steps** spent in transient state $j$, given that the attack starts in transient state $i$. Because $N = I + Q + \dots$, the starting step counts: $N_{ii} \ge 1$.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Expected Time to Resolution ($\mathbf{t}$)

The expected number of steps before the chain is absorbed, from any starting state, is

$$\mathbf{t} = N \mathbf{1}, \qquad t_i = \sum_{j=1}^t N_{ij}$$

This is the expected time to **resolution of any kind**, including containment. A shorter time to resolution can mean faster containment (good) or faster detonation (bad), so it is not by itself a measure of adversary dwell. Each stage $j$ has its own mean step duration $h_j$ (hours), giving

$$\mathbf{T} = N \mathbf{h}$$

In this document every $h_j = \Delta\tau = 4$ hours, so $\mathbf{T} = 4\,\mathbf{t}$. The 4-hour step is an assumption. Rescaling hours to a different step is not a valid sensitivity check, because per-step probabilities only have meaning for the step length they were elicited for: a 1-hour step with the same $P$ would imply a SOC that contains attacks four times faster per hour. A continuous-time formulation (rates per hour) removes this coupling.

The two layers of the model separate cleanly. The absorption probabilities $B$ depend only on the **embedded jump chain**, meaning each transient row with its self-loop removed and the remaining entries renormalized. The self-loops and $\Delta\tau$ affect timing only. The code below verifies this.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Absorption Probabilities ($B$)

The matrix $B \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times r}$ gives the probability that an attack starting in transient state $i$ ends in absorbing state $k$:

$$B = N R, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^r B_{ik} = 1 \;\; \text{for all } i$$

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Conditional Time to Each Outcome

The expected time to resolution, conditional on ending in outcome $k$, has the closed form

$$E[T \mid \text{start } i,\ \text{absorbed in } k] = \frac{\left(N\,(\mathbf{h} \circ B_{\cdot k})\right)_i}{B_{ik}}$$

where $\circ$ is the elementwise product. For a catastrophic outcome, $B_{\cdot k}$ is replaced by $B_{\cdot A2} + B_{\cdot A3}$. This is the time metric that bears on response timelines, because it separates "time until containment" from "time until a catastrophic outcome, given that one occurs."

## Parameterization and Assumptions {#parameterization-and-assumptions}

The model has six transient states (kill-chain-style stages) and three absorbing states (outcomes). **Transition probabilities are expert-elicited, illustrative values.** They are not calibrated to any cited benchmark, and they were typed in by hand: no asset graph, PageRank, CVSS weighting, or loss distribution is computed. Each transient row gives the per-step probability, from a given stage, of staying (self-loop), advancing, regressing, or ending in an absorbing outcome. The baseline and the improved-posture matrices are two hand-set matrices; the improved posture is not derived from any specific control (MFA, EDR, or Zero Trust).

Assumptions that the results depend on:

-   **Markov property.** "Starting at $S_i$" and "having reached $S_i$" are treated as identical, and the per-step detection hazard is constant. An attacker who reached $S_3$ after 30 hours of slow movement has the same future as one who arrived in 4 hours; real detection probability can build up with time and noise.
-   **Geometric holding times and a single step length.** Each stage has a geometric holding time with a step length of $\Delta\tau = 4$ hours for all stages. Because the self-loops are small (0.05–0.15), an attacker leaves each stage after a single step 85–95% of the time. The model's timing is therefore close to deterministic, and no catastrophic outcome can occur in under 16 hours (see [Time-to-Outcome Distributions](#time-to-outcome-distributions)). Real stages differ by orders of magnitude, from sub-hour lateral movement to multi-week dwell, so the implied time scale is a hypothetical, not a benchmark. The per-step probabilities are tied to the 4-hour step, so changing the step without re-eliciting them is not a valid sensitivity check.
-   **S1 entry event.** S1 begins when a user **engages** with a phishing lure (opens a malicious attachment, follows a malicious link, or submits credentials), not when a message is delivered. Filtered and ignored messages are outside the model. Under the baseline, 72% of S1 attacks eventually reach S2 (0.65 / 0.90). That is plausible only for this engaged cohort and would be far too high for delivered messages. Time spent in S1 is time to resolution, not dwell time in the industry sense, because the adversary is not yet inside the environment.
-   **Outcome space.** "Contained / Terminated" (A1) includes automated filtering, user non-engagement, abandoned attacks, and SOC action; it should not be read as "SOC wins." A2 and A3 are mutually exclusive here, although double extortion (exfiltrate, then encrypt) is common; "catastrophic" counts only the first catastrophic outcome, and the A2/A3 split is not meaningful by itself. A1 is also treated as permanent, although containment is often partial and adversaries can re-enter through persistence or harvested credentials, so containment probabilities are upper bounds on durable containment.
-   **Graph structure.** Only S5 and S6 can reach A2/A3, and S6 has no regression edge, so catastrophic probability is largely P(reach S5/S6) times the S5/S6 exit split. The single back-edge S4 → S3 (0.05) is an illustrative attacker-backtracking edge with no empirical basis, kept in both regimes. The zeros from S2–S4 into A2 are also a strong claim: data theft from file shares or SaaS does not require domain dominance.
-   **Containment share by depth.** Conditional on leaving a stage, the share of exits that go to A1 falls from about 0.28–0.29 at S1–S2 to about 0.10–0.11 at S5–S6. That encodes a claim that defenders get worse at stopping attacks as the attacks become noisier. Deep stages usually generate the richest telemetry; what shrinks is the time left to respond. A continuous-time formulation, in which detection and progression are competing rates, can reproduce falling containment at depth without this assumption.
-   **Single entry vector.** Phishing is the only way in. Exploits were the most common initial infection vector in Mandiant's 2024 investigations, at 33% ([M-Trends 2025](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/m-trends-2025-en.pdf)). In 56% of Sophos's 2024 cases, adversaries logged in with valid credentials rather than breaking in ([Sophos](https://www.sophos.com/en-us/press/press-releases/2025/04/sophos-report-56-sophos-ir-and-mdr-cases-adversaries-logged-instead)). Valid-credential entry would skip S1 and start at S2 or S3.

### Time-to-Outcome Distributions and the Structural Floor {#time-to-outcome-distributions}

Means hide the shape of the timing. The cumulative incidence function (CIF) for outcome $k$, starting from stage $i$, is the probability that the attack has ended in $k$ by step $s$:

$$\mathrm{CIF}_k(s) = \sum_{u=0}^{s-1} \mathbf{e}_i^\top Q^u R_{\cdot k}$$

It is exact in closed form from matrix powers, with no simulation needed.

**How to read the plot.** Each line shows, for an attack that starts at S1, the chance that it has ended in a given way by a given hour. The green line is containment and the red line a catastrophic outcome (data theft or ransomware); the left panel is the baseline and the right the improved posture. The lines climb in steps because the model moves in 4-hour steps. The dashed line marks hour `r floor_cat_h`.

**What the plot tells you.** No catastrophic outcome can happen before hour `r floor_cat_h`, because the shortest route to one takes four 4-hour steps; this floor comes from the model's design, not from attacker behavior. Under the baseline, the chance of catastrophe reaches `r pct1(p_cat_by_24h)` by hour 24 and levels off at `r pct1(res$cat_b[1])`. Under the improved posture it levels off at only `r pct1(res$cat_h[1])`. Real attacks can move much faster or slower than this fixed schedule allows.

**In more detail**

**A hard floor.** The chain moves one edge per 4-hour step, and the shortest path to a catastrophic outcome from S1 (S1 → S2 → S4 → S6 → A3) has four edges. No catastrophic outcome can therefore occur before `r floor_cat_h` hours. By 24 hours, the probability of a catastrophic outcome is `r pct1(p_cat_by_24h)`, out of a lifetime `r pct1(res$cat_b[1])`.

**An almost fixed schedule.** Conditional on a catastrophic outcome, the 10th, 50th, 90th, and 99th percentiles of time from S1 are `r paste(q_cat_b, collapse = ", ")` hours, with a coefficient of variation of `r sprintf("%.2f", cov_cat_b)`. Conditional on containment, the median and 90th percentile are `r paste(q_cont_b, collapse = " and ")` hours. Each stage is left after one step `r pct1(min(p_leave_first_step))`–`r pct1(max(p_leave_first_step))` of the time, so time to catastrophe is close to (number of stages traversed) × 4 hours.

**Benchmarks fall outside this range on both sides.** The average eCrime breakout time, from the first compromised host to another, was 48 minutes in 2024 ([CrowdStrike 2025 Global Threat Report](https://www.crowdstrike.com/en-us/blog/crowdstrike-2025-global-threat-report-findings/)). Sophos reports a median of 11 hours from an attacker's initial action to the first attempt on Active Directory ([Sophos 2025 Active Adversary Report](https://www.sophos.com/en-us/blog/2025-sophos-active-adversary-report)). Mandiant reports a 2024 median dwell time of 11 days overall and 6 days for ransomware intrusions ([M-Trends 2025](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/m-trends-2025-en.pdf)). The discrete 4-hour chain can represent neither the sub-hour tail nor the multi-week tail, so its time outputs should be read as a restatement of the step assumption.
